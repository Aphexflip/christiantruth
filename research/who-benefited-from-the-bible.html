History • power • biblical scholarship

Who benefited
from the Bible?

Was the Bible created to control people? The evidence supports something more complicated and more useful: biblical texts formed over centuries inside real systems of kingship, temples, priesthoods, scribal literacy, national crisis, and later empire. Some texts clearly strengthened institutions. Others attacked those institutions.

Best-supported conclusion: there was no single mastermind who invented “the Bible” as a control scheme. But sacred texts can function as unusually powerful social technology: they can make laws, borders, institutions, warfare, identity rules, and political programs appear to carry divine rather than merely human authority.

Who could benefit?

Benefit does not prove fraud. A king, priest, or scribe could sincerely believe a religious program and still gain power from it. The historical question is what the text or institution actually accomplished.

STRONG CASE

Kings and reformers

Deuteronomy’s demand for one authorized place of sacrifice aligns with the centralizing reform attributed to King Josiah in 2 Kings 22–23. Centralization strengthened Jerusalem as Judah’s religious center.

STRONG CASE

Temple institutions

When legitimate sacrifice is concentrated in one sanctuary, that sanctuary gains prestige, traffic, resources, and political importance while local shrines lose legitimacy.

STRONG CASE

Priestly groups

Priestly texts regulate sacrifice, purity, sacred space, festivals, and hereditary religious roles. That makes religious specialists structurally important — though biblical law codes disagree about priestly authority.

STRONG CASE

Scribes

Ancient Judah was not a mass-literacy society. The people trained to write, copy, preserve, interpret, and publicly teach authoritative texts possessed unusual cultural power.

STRONG CASE

Exilic leaders and communities

After Jerusalem fell in 586 BCE, covenant theology could explain catastrophe as punishment for disobedience rather than defeat of Israel’s God. That helped preserve identity under foreign domination.

LATER PERIOD

Christian emperors and bishops

The New Testament began before a Christian state existed. The church–state power argument becomes much stronger after Constantine and later emperors made Christianity an imperial institution.

The clearest case: Josiah

If you want one example where theology and political centralization visibly intersect, start here.

Deuteronomy 12 says sacrifice is to occur at the place chosen by Israel’s God. 2 Kings 22–23 says a “book of the law” was discovered during Temple repairs under King Josiah, after which Josiah destroyed outlying shrines, suppressed rival forms of worship, and centralized authorized sacrifice.

For generations, historical-critical scholarship has connected the core of Deuteronomy with this late-seventh-century BCE reform environment. The exact editing history remains debated, but the institutional effect is easy to see:

many local shrines → one authorized center
regional religious variation → standardized covenant worship
local cultic authority → Jerusalem-centered authority

This does not mean “Josiah invented Judaism.” It means sacred law and state reform could reinforce one another.

What about God ordering slaughter?

These passages are not internet inventions. The texts themselves portray extreme violence as divinely authorized. The historical issue is what that rhetoric meant and what it did.

Deuteronomy 20:16–18

The law commands total destruction in specified Canaanite cities and explicitly connects the policy to preventing Israel from adopting rival religious practices.

Function: extreme insider/outsider boundary making.

1 Samuel 15:3

Saul is commanded to destroy Amalek, including children and livestock. His failure to carry out the command completely becomes the theological basis for his rejection as king.

Function: even royal discretion is subordinate to divine command.

Joshua 6:21

Jericho’s destruction is presented as part of God’s delivery of the land to Israel.

Function: territorial possession is framed as divine entitlement rather than merely human conquest.

Numbers 31:17–18

The Midian narrative combines warfare, vengeance, sexual/religious boundary policing, captive populations, and distribution of war booty.

Function: violence and social boundaries receive sacred authorization.

Important historical context: Israel was not unique in describing war as commanded or enabled by a national god. The Moabite Mesha Stele credits Chemosh with victory over Israel, while Assyrian royal inscriptions and reliefs present overwhelming military domination under divine and royal authority. This context makes biblical holy-war rhetoric historically recognizable without making it morally acceptable.

Why divine authority is politically powerful

Law becomes sacred

A disputed human policy can become a command from the creator. Disobedience becomes more than illegality: it becomes sin.

Land becomes promised

Territory can be framed not simply as conquered or inherited, but as divinely granted.

Opponents become cosmic

Political or religious outsiders can be recast as threats to sacred order rather than ordinary rivals.

Institutions become necessary

Temples, priesthoods, scribes, and authorized interpreters gain authority when access to sacred truth runs through them.

Disaster gets explained

Defeat need not disprove the deity. It can be interpreted as the deity punishing the community for insufficient obedience.

Identity becomes durable

Law, ritual, story, and sacred time can preserve a community through exile and foreign rule. Control and cultural survival can coexist.

What not to claim

Weak claimBetter claim
“A king invented the Bible to control everyone.”The Bible formed over centuries, but important layers intersect with identifiable royal, temple, priestly, scribal, national, and later imperial interests.
“Constantine created Christianity and picked the Bible at Nicaea.”Christianity and Christian texts long predate Constantine; his reign instead marks a major transition toward imperial patronage and political church power.
“Every biblical law only benefits elites.”Biblical texts contain elite-serving institutions alongside restrictions on kings, concern for vulnerable people, debt rules, and fierce criticism of rulers and priests.
“If religion benefits someone, they knowingly made it up.”Institutional benefit and sincere belief can coexist. Historical evidence rarely lets us read an ancient author’s private motives with certainty.

Verdict

“The Bible was invented to control people” is historically too crude. There was no single author, government, or moment of invention.

But biblical religion was never outside politics and power. Sacred texts could centralize worship, structure priestly authority, reinforce scribal expertise, define insiders and outsiders, justify land claims and warfare, explain national catastrophe, and later support state-backed religious institutions.

The better mental model is: scripture can operate as a technology of social organization. That technology can preserve communities and restrain rulers — and it can also make institutions, hierarchies, borders, wars, and rules harder to challenge by presenting them as the will of God.
